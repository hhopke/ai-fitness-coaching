## How the confidence labels in this file were assigned

The position stand does **not** use this repo's CONSENSUS / CONTESTED /
PRACTITIONER vocabulary. It grades each outcome as impacts (✔), does not
impact (✖) or cannot determine (?), with a **quality of evidence (QoE)
percentage**: the average QoE across contributing reviews divided by 4, where
each review's QoE combines design (meta-analysis yes/no) with AMSTAR quality.
The mapping used here:

| Position stand grading | Label used here |
| --- | --- |
| ✔ or ✖ decided, multiple contributing reviews, QoE ≥ ~60% | **[CONSENSUS]** |
| ✔ or ✖ decided on a single review, or QoE < ~60%, or contributing reviews split in direction | **[CONTESTED]** |
| ? cannot determine / ND (no data) | **[NO EVIDENCE]** — stated as absence, not as a null |
| Authors' own proposal, flagged in the text as theirs and not an output of the synthesis | **[PRACTITIONER]** |

Every numeric claim below is the position stand's own. Where a number is the
authors' inference rather than a synthesis output, it is marked.

## TL;DR

- **137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants.** Versus no exercise,
  resistance training improves strength, hypertrophy, power, muscular
  endurance, contraction velocity, gait speed, balance, chair stand and timed
  up-and-go **[CONSENSUS]**.
- **Few prescription variables change the outcome.** Strength: load ≥80% 1RM
  (dose-response), ≥2 sessions/wk, full range of motion, 2–3 sets/session,
  exercise placed at the **beginning** of the session. Hypertrophy: volume
  (≥10 sets/muscle/wk, dose-response) and eccentric overload **[CONSENSUS]**.
- **Nulls:** training to momentary muscular failure, machine vs free weight,
  exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension, blood flow
  restriction, inter-set rest, time of day and periodization did **not**
  consistently affect outcomes **[CONSENSUS]**.
- **Load and volume dissociate by outcome.** Strength is load-driven and
  volume-saturating (~2–3 sets/exercise). Hypertrophy is volume-driven and
  load-indifferent across 30%–100% 1RM **[CONSENSUS]**.
- **Novice bias is acknowledged by the authors**: much of the synthesised
  evidence comes from inexperienced trainees. This is the most important
  caveat for a trained athlete.

## Inclusion criteria

From Table 1 and the eligibility section, summarised:

- **Population:** healthy adults ≥18 yr without defined disease; obesity,
  sarcopenia and physical frailty excluded. Any training status.
- **Intervention:** resistance training of **≥6 wk** (included range
  **6–52 wk**) with **≥12 exposures**. Co-interventions had to be given to
  both arms.
- **Comparator:** a distinct RT prescription (FITT-VP), a non-exercise
  control, or a sham-exercise control.
- **Outcome:** pre-to-post change in muscle function (strength, power,
  endurance, contraction velocity, physical function) or hypertrophy.
- **Design:** systematic reviews (including overviews) of randomized trials.
- **Strength definition:** by default, isotonic **1RM in the training mode**.
- **Search:** six databases to **October 2024**; 5,751 records after
  de-duplication; 137 reviews included; AMSTAR **1–9 of 11**.
- **Age:** reviews not separated by age; recommendations presented as
  applying across adult ages.

### Scope limits the authors state

- **Training status.** Most included reviews examined novices; the authors
  note prior work found training experience had minimal impact, but state
  that "much of the evidence synthesized here is from inexperienced
  trainees." This caveat governs every number in this file and travels with
  any figure applied to a trained athlete.
- **Body region.** The authors state "the available evidence did not compare
  distinct body regions or muscle groups." The position stand therefore says
  **nothing** about which exercise to choose, region-specific growth, or
  transfer between lifts. That is an absence of comparison, not a null.

## Section A — Strength

### A1. What enhances strength
**[CONSENSUS]** Versus non-exercising control, strength improved with
standard RT (26 reviews; n = 23,204; QoE = 73%), circuit RT (3; n = 843;
QoE = 92%), elastic band RT (2; n = 1,921; QoE = 63%), home-based RT (2;
n = 892; QoE = 50%) and velocity-based RT (2; n = 870; QoE = 50%).
Unilateral RT improved strength in the untrained contralateral limb (2;
n = 1,194; QoE = 88%).

**[CONSENSUS] Prescription variables that moved strength** (Tables 4 and 6):

| Variable | Finding | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Load** | **≥80% 1RM**, dose-response | ✔ 6 reviews; n = 6,574; QoE = 79% |
| **Volume** | **2–3 sets/session** (multiple > single) | ✔ 7 reviews; n = 5,633; QoE = 71% |
| **Frequency** | **≥2 sessions/wk** | ✔ 4 reviews; n = 3,531; QoE = 69% |
| **Exercise order** | at the **beginning** of the session | ✔ 4 reviews; n = 941; QoE = 88% |
| **Range of motion** | **full** ROM > partial | ✔ 2 reviews; n = 1,262; QoE = 50% |
| **Eccentric flywheel** | > standard RT | ✔ 1 review; n = 332; QoE = 75% |

**[CONTESTED]** Range of motion rests on QoE = 50% across two reviews; the
flywheel finding on a single review, and the authors note flywheel RT
usually includes eccentric overload, so the two effects cannot be separated.

**[CONSENSUS] Frequency has no established dose-response and is
volume-dependent.** The effect diminishes when volume is equated; the
authors state ≥2 d/wk and that the upper limit cannot be determined from
their analysis.

### A2. What did NOT affect strength
**[CONSENSUS]** Strength was **not** affected by:

- contractions **to muscle failure/fatigue** (✖ 3 reviews; n = 1,371;
  QoE = 83%)
- **free weights vs machines** (✖ 1; n = 683; QoE = 75%)
- **unstable vs stable surfaces** (✖ 1; n = 438; QoE = 75%)
- **time under tension**, <2 s vs >2 s contractions (✖ 1; n = 509;
  QoE = 75%)
- **time of day** (✖ 2; n = 430; QoE = 75%)
- **inter-set rest**, <1 min vs >1 min (✖ 2; n = 982; QoE = 63%)
- **contraction type**, eccentric vs concentric (✖ 1; n = 1,051; QoE = 75%)
- **power training technique** (✖ 2; n = 670; QoE = 75%)
- **set structure**, cluster or complex (✖ 3; n = 1,837; QoE = 83%)

**[NO EVIDENCE]** Undetermined for strength: blood flow restriction,
Olympic-style weightlifting, intrasession autoregulation, **varied exercise
selection** (1; n = 198; QoE = 50%), **periodization**, **concurrent
training**.

### A3. Volume dose-response for strength
**[CONSENSUS]** One set beats none and two beat one, but the optimal number
of sets cannot be ascertained. A cited meta-regression shows a dose-response
that **plateaus with diminishing returns beyond ~2–3 sets/exercise**. Stated
floor: at least two sets per exercise.

**[PRACTITIONER]** That benefit diminishes with each set beyond two is
framed by the authors as a hypothesis, not a finding.

## Section B — Hypertrophy

### B1. What enhances hypertrophy
**[CONSENSUS]** Versus control, hypertrophy improved with standard RT (12
reviews; n = 14,924; QoE = 79%), circuit RT and elastic band RT.

Between prescriptions, **two variables** enhanced hypertrophy:

| Variable | Finding | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Volume** | **≥10 sets/muscle group/wk**, dose-response | ✔ 5 reviews; n = 2,267; QoE = 50% |
| **Contraction type** | **eccentric contractions / eccentric overload** | ✔ 1 review; n = 868; QoE = 75% |

**[CONTESTED]** Both are weakly supported relative to the strength findings:
the volume finding averages QoE = 50% and one contributing review (n = 555;
QoE = 75%) pointed the other way; contraction type rests on one review, with
two more (n = 92; QoE = 50%) undetermined.

**[CONSENSUS] Volume dose-response:** the cited meta-regression shows
diminishing returns **beyond ~18–20 weekly sets**.

### B2. What did NOT affect hypertrophy
**[CONSENSUS]** Hypertrophy was **not** affected by:

- **load**, across **30%–100% 1RM** (✖ 8 reviews; n = 5,340; QoE = 66%)
- **frequency**, 1 d/wk vs >5 d/wk **at equated volume** (✖ 4; n = 3,177;
  QoE = 63%)
- contractions **to muscle failure/fatigue** (✖ 4; n = 800; QoE = 69%)
- **blood flow restriction** (✖ 2; n = 587; QoE = 75%)
- **variable loading** within a repetition (✖ 1; n = 408; QoE = 50%)
- **time under tension**, 0.5 s vs 8 s repetitions (✖ 3; n = 806; QoE = 58%)
- **power training technique** (✖ 1; n = 336; QoE = 75%)
- **periodization**, linear / undulating / none (✖ 4; n = 1,414; QoE = 63%)
- **exercise order** within the session (✖ 4; n = 759; QoE = 81%)

**[NO EVIDENCE]** Undetermined for hypertrophy: flywheel vs standard,
machine vs free weight, **single- vs multi-joint**, time of day, inter-set
rest, **range of motion** (? 3; n = 276; QoE = 33%), set structure,
intrasession autoregulation, **varied exercise selection**, concurrent
training.

### B3. The load/volume dissociation
**[CONSENSUS]** **Strength is load-driven and saturates on volume at ~2–3
sets/exercise; hypertrophy is volume-driven and indifferent to load from 30%
to 100% 1RM.** The authors note load has repeatedly been shown to affect
strength more than other adaptations. These are separate levers; optimising
one does not optimise the other.

## Section C — Failure, effort, and progression

### C1. Training to failure
**[CONSENSUS]** Sets to momentary muscular failure do **not** enhance
strength, hypertrophy or power; the authors conclude failure "is not
necessary for benefits to occur." They add it may be inadvisable for some
populations, e.g. older individuals, on vascular and form-related injury
grounds.

**[PRACTITIONER]** The authors' own proposal: an adequate stimulus is still
required, achievable through near-failure sets or a target of **2–3
repetitions in reserve (RIR)**. Explicitly framed as theirs.

**[NO EVIDENCE]** The authors state there is "insufficient evidence to
quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets." The 2–3 RIR figure is a
practitioner target, not a synthesised threshold. Any rule specifying an RPE
or RIR band should say so.

### C2. Progressive overload
**[CONTESTED]** Progression is demoted relative to the 2009 stand: the
authors state "progression is not necessary to achieve beneficial outcomes",
and that increasing the stimulus is likely required only for those seeking
continued longer-term progress.

**[PRACTITIONER]** The authors frame continued progression as a personal,
individualised decision.

What progression is said to act on: load, volume, training frequency,
exercise selection, or duration. Proximity to failure is not on that list,
and is separately found not to enhance outcomes (C1).

### C3. Periodization and variation
**[CONSENSUS for hypertrophy / NO EVIDENCE for strength]** Periodization did
not affect hypertrophy (✖ 4 reviews; n = 1,414; QoE = 63%). For strength it
could not be determined, though one review slightly favoured periodized
programs at equated volume.

**[CONSENSUS]** With appropriate progressive overload, periodization is not
significantly superior to non-periodized training and is "less important
than previously hypothesized" for healthy adults.

**[NO EVIDENCE]** **Varied exercise selection** undetermined for strength
(1 review; n = 198; QoE = 50%) and hypertrophy. Neither supported nor
refuted.

## Section D — Power and muscular endurance

### D1. Power
**[CONSENSUS]** RT improved power vs control (4 reviews; n = 1,001;
QoE = 63%). Power was enhanced by:

- **moderate loads, 30%–70% 1RM** (✔ 2; n = 1,980; QoE = 75%)
- **power RT**, maximal-velocity concentric phase (✔ 2; n = 863; QoE = 75%)
- **Olympic-style weightlifting** vs standard RT
- **eccentric flywheel** RT (✔ 1; n = 235; QoE = 75%)
- **low-to-moderate volume, repetitions × sets ≤ 24** (✔ 1; n = 454;
  QoE = 75%)

Not affected by set structure. **[NO EVIDENCE]** for failure, unstable
surfaces, or concurrent training on power.

**[CONSENSUS]** Power RT improved **multicomponent physical function**,
walking performance and SPPB where standard RT did not — the clearest
functional-transfer finding.

### D2. Muscular endurance
**[CONSENSUS]** RT improved muscular endurance vs control. **[NO EVIDENCE]**
for every prescription variable examined (load, unstable surfaces, power
training, volume, set structure). Test method (absolute vs relative load)
was not accounted for.

## Section E — What this position stand does NOT cover

Absence is the operative finding for several rules. The outcome set is:
strength, hypertrophy, power, muscular endurance, contraction velocity, and
physical function (gait speed, TUG, chair stand, balance, stair climbing,
multicomponent function, SPPB, walking, running, jumping, change of
direction, functional reach).

It has **no** outcome for, and so says nothing about:

- **Tendon** stiffness, Young's modulus, cross-sectional area, or strain.
- **Bone** mineral density.
- **Injury incidence or prevention** as an outcome. (Adverse events are
  discussed; injury-risk reduction is not a synthesised outcome.)
- **Mortality, CVD or cancer dose-response.** Cited in the introduction, no
  minutes-per-week dose-response synthesised.
- **Exercise choice or region-specific / architectural growth.** See the
  body-region scope limit above.
- **Exercise-specific strength transfer** between named lifts.
- **EMG or activation** as a selection criterion.
- **Climbing, finger tissue, or any sport-specific application.**

## Section F — Out of scope for the strength rules, recorded for completeness

### F1. Concurrent training
**[NO EVIDENCE]** Concurrent aerobic + RT was **indeterminate for all three
primary outcomes**: strength (↑ 2 reviews, n = 725, QoE = 75%; ? 1, n = 119,
QoE = 25%; ↓ 3, n = 354, QoE = 67% — direction split), hypertrophy (? 3;
n = 351; QoE = 67%) and power. No position on interference, session
separation, ordering between modalities, or frequency caps.

### F2. Masters / older athletes
**[CONSENSUS]** Reviews were not separated by age. Across >38,000
participants (>6,700 RT, >11,000 older adults), exercise did not increase
serious adverse events, and non-serious adverse event risk did not differ
from aerobic exercise; the authors conclude RT is safe for healthy adults of
all ages. Participation estimates in older persons range 1%–40%.

**[CONSENSUS]** Power RT specifically improved multicomponent physical
function and walking performance.

**[PRACTITIONER]** The authors advise failure training may be inadvisable in
older individuals.

### F3. Exercise selection by body region — the authors' own proposal
**[PRACTITIONER]** Stated "in the view of the authors", with no evidence
base: applying the recommendations to upper/lower × push/pull (four regions)
is sufficient to target major muscle groups, optionally splitting upper-body
work into horizontal and vertical (six regions).

## Limitations the position stand states about itself

- Overviews of reviews cannot support statistical comparison of individual
  variables or interventions; network meta-analysis is named as the method
  that could.
- Overlap between reviews was quantified (corrected covered area) **only for
  strength**: moderate, 6%–10%. Other outcomes carry unquantified overlap.
- Weekly **volume load** (load × reps × sets × exercises × sessions), which
  the authors regard as the most complete representation of dose, was rarely
  considered in included reviews.
- Underlying RCTs are often small, poorly randomised, unregistered, and
  weak on adverse-event reporting. Pooled GRADE was not possible.
- Insufficient evidence to recommend emerging strategies; **minimal-dose RT
  programs** are named as needing investigation. The position stand does
  **not** validate a minimum effective dose protocol.
- Exact RIR and perceived-exertion targets cannot be quantified.
- Frequency's upper limit cannot be determined.
- The authors caution that the SPPB null likely reflects sparse evidence
  rather than a true null — a warning against reading ✖ and ? as
  demonstrated absence of effect.
