# State-of-the-Art Evidence Review: Climbing/Bouldering Load & Injury + Resistance Training for Injury Prevention

*Reference material for an automated training-planning system. Athlete profile: primary endurance = cycling; primary discipline = bouldering/climbing; dedicated strength training; occasional low-intensity running. Objective: longevity-led, performance secondary. This extends a prior endurance-sport review (cycling/running/swimming load-adaptation, intensity distribution, periodization, taper, longevity, injury prevention) and covers only what that review omitted. Every substantive claim is labeled [CONSENSUS], [CONTESTED], or [PRACTITIONER].*

---

## TL;DR

- **Climbing load must be tracked on a separate finger/upper-limb mechanical axis and cannot be summed into endurance load.** The only validated internal-load metric (session-RPE) captures bouldering worst of all disciplines (validated r=0.40), and no validated finger-specific external-load metric, grade-weighting, or climbing↔endurance conversion exists.
- **Set resistance-training dose to the injury-prevention target (dose-dependent, ~66% injury-risk reduction), not the mortality-minimum (~30–60 min/wk).** Prioritize the tissues climbing does NOT train — lower-body/knee-tendon and shoulder-girdle/push — because hard bouldering already delivers a near-maximal finger/pulling stimulus that risks being double-counted (over-loading fingers) or wrongly assumed to cover all upper-body strength (under-prescribing).
- **The climbing evidence base is weak** (small cross-sectional/retrospective studies, self-report, two injury-prevention RCTs from a single cohort, sub-n=30 finger-training trials, heavy practitioner-convention corpus); the resistance-training mortality/injury evidence is strong (meta-analyses, large cohorts) but almost none is climbing- or cyclist-specific, so prevention claims for cycling knee overuse and climbing antagonist training are **extrapolated, not directly demonstrated**.

---

## Key Findings

1. **Load quantification:** Session-RPE is validated in climbing but is discipline-sensitive and weakest for bouldering. There is **no** validated external-load metric and **no** currency shared with endurance load.
2. **Epidemiology:** Overuse dominates (up to 68% of adult injuries); fingers > shoulder > elbow. Bouldering carries ~5× the roped-climbing incidence rate (1.47 vs 0.29/1000 h). Finger pulley (A2>A4) is the signature injury.
3. **Load management:** **No climbing equivalent of running's single-session spike / ACWR threshold exists.** Previous injury and climbing-in-pain are the strongest modifiable risk markers.
4. **Tissue timescales:** Neural/strength finger gains bank in weeks; structural pulley/tendon adaptation lags into months-to-years — the mechanistic basis of finger overuse when load is ramped on the muscular timescale.
5. **Hangboarding** reliably raises finger strength/endurance in trained climbers, but protocol specifics and the beginner ban are convention; the dominant injury mechanism is the total-finger-load frequency spike, not the tool.
6. **Resistance-training doses diverge:** mortality-optimal (~30–60 min/wk, ceiling ~120–140), injury-prevention (dose-dependent, no ceiling), performance (highest). One dose does not satisfy all three.

## Details

## SECTION A — CLIMBING AND BOULDERING

### A1. Load quantification

- **[CONSENSUS] Session-RPE is the only validated internal-load metric in climbing.** Validated against Edwards HR-based TRIMP in 15 high-level Italian climbers (6F/9M, age 20.4±3.0). Whole-group correlation general-RPE vs Edwards r=0.828 (p<0.001); local (forearm) RPE r=0.647. By discipline: **bouldering r=0.40 (moderate, p<0.01)**; lead r=0.61; training r=0.66. With local forearm RPE, lead r=0.91, training r=0.81. **Interpretation: sRPE captures bouldering load worst of all disciplines** because bouldering is short/intermittent and dominated by local (finger/forearm) rather than systemic strain — the facet the planning system most needs is the facet sRPE tracks least well.
- **[CONTESTED/PRACTITIONER] External-load metrics have no validation.** Counts of attempts, boulders, moves, time-on-wall, and grade-weighted schemes are used by coaches and apps but none has an established criterion-validity study. "Density" (moves per unit time) and grade-weighted volume are practitioner constructs.
- **[CONSENSUS] Climbing load cannot be expressed in an endurance currency.** Endurance internal load (TRIMP, TSS, kJ) is cardiovascular/metabolic; the binding climbing constraint is localized connective-tissue and finger-flexor mechanical strain, which no cardiovascular metric captures. **The planning system must track climbing load on a separate axis** and must NOT sum it into a single systemic load score with cycling/running.
- **Open questions:** No validated finger-specific external-load metric; no validated grade-weighting; no established method to combine finger mechanical load with systemic endurance load.

### A2. Injury epidemiology

- **[CONSENSUS] Overuse dominates; fingers then shoulder most prevalent.** Fasulo et al. (2026), *"Epidemiology of Musculoskeletal Injuries Among Climbers—A Systematic Review,"* J. Funct. Morphol. Kinesiol. 11(1):19 (7 studies, >4000 climbers): "overuse injuries constitute the dominant pattern in adult population, with one study reporting they comprise 68% of all injuries."
- **[CONSENSUS] Incidence rates (per 1000 h), discipline-separated:**
  - General climbing population (Swedish survey): **4.2/1000 h**, 93% overuse; inflammatory finger/wrist damage most common.
  - Competition World Cup: overall **0.74/1000 h**; **bouldering 1.47 vs rope 0.29/1000 h** — bouldering ~5× roped (also reproduced by Backe et al.: boulderers 1.47 vs indoor wall climbers 0.29).
  - Competitive youth (USA): **2.7/1000 h**, hand/finger most frequent, bouldering > sport/lead.
  - Acute indoor risk very low: **0.02/1000 h** (515,337 visits, Germany, 5 yr; 30 climber injuries). Older estimates 0.027 (Limb) and 0.079 (Schöffl/Winkelmann).
  - Outdoor trad historically much higher (~37.5/1000 h, 1980s Yosemite).
  - Systematic-review ranges: incidence proportion 26–300 injuries/100 participants; point/period prevalence 15–81% (windows vary from 6 months to whole career).
- **[CONSENSUS] Finger pulley (A2>A4) is the signature injury.** Most common overuse injury; annular pulley ruptures + flexor tendon injuries account for up to 33% of all injuries in some cohorts. Per biomechanical testing cited by The Climbing Doctor (Schöffl/Vigouroux data): "When crimping (as opposed to open-hand gripping) the force on the A4 pulley increases 3.9 times and the force on the A2 pulley increases 31.5 times." Mechanism: crimp, often audible "pop." Per Schweizer et al.: "The force of the finger flexors in recreational climbers could theoretically be greater than 400N which is very close or beyond the maximum strength of the A2 pulley of 407N seen in cadaver studies."
- **[CONSENSUS] Shoulder ≈17% of climbing injuries.** Per Cole/Schöffl et al. review "Rock Climbing Injuries: Acute and Chronic Repetitive Trauma": the shoulder comprises approximately 17% of all injuries, and chronic shoulder pain is seen in approximately 33% of elite climbers; Schöffl survey found SLAP tears 33%, external impingement 26%, anterior dislocations with labral tears 10%. Also long-head biceps involvement; more common in older climbers and overhead/dynamic moves.
- **[CONSENSUS] Elbow: medial epicondylalgia ("climber's elbow", flexor-pronator/FCR) most common; lateral also occurs.** Described as under-reported in the literature but very common clinically; a leading cause of time off.
- **[CONSENSUS] Acute injuries: bouldering falls → ankle (lateral sprain, fracture) and knee (meniscus, from heel-hooks/drop-knees/high-steps).** Falls onto the mat dominate the acute mechanism.
- **[CONSENSUS] Adolescents: finger epiphyseal (growth-plate) stress fractures, Salter-Harris III at the dorsal base of the middle phalanx.** Per Fasulo et al. (2026): "a notably elevated risk was identified for adolescent climbers (ages 15–19), with one study reporting an odds ratio of 11.3." A pediatric issue, not directly applicable to an adult athlete but relevant if planning youth.
- **Open questions:** True denominators poor (self-report, recall bias); prospective outdoor bouldering incidence sparse; SLAP prevalence in recreational climbers not well quantified.

### A3. Risk factors and load-management evidence

- **[CONSENSUS/CONTESTED] Associated with higher overuse-injury risk** (anchored to Quarmby et al. 2023, Frontiers Sports Act. Living 5:1269870, 34 studies, 1,183 records): "Higher climbing intensity, bouldering, reduced grip/finger strength, use of a 'crimp' grip, and previous injury were associated with an increased risk of overuse injury." Youth/competition data add: returning to climb while in pain, finger taping (likely a marker of existing issues), more hours/session and hours/year, higher bouldering difficulty, and unsupervised climbing.
- **[CONTESTED] Conflicting/no association** (Quarmby et al.): "BMI/body weight, warm up/cool downs, stretching, taping and hydration were not associated" with overuse risk; training volume, age/years of experience, and sex were conflicting across studies.
- **[CONSENSUS — absence] No climbing equivalent of running's single-session spike / acute:chronic workload ratio finding exists.** No validated ramp-rate, ACWR, or progression-rate threshold has been established for climbing or finger load. Practitioner rules ("increase grade/volume gradually," "10% rule") are extrapolated, not climbing-validated. **No evidence exists for a specific climbing load-progression threshold.**
- **Open questions:** No prospective ramp-rate study; grade-progression-rate as an injury predictor untested; interaction of hangboard volume with climbing volume on injury unquantified.

### A4. Tissue adaptation timescales

- **[CONSENSUS] Muscle/neural finger-strength gains outpace connective-tissue structural adaptation.** Neural adaptations (max hangs) occur in weeks; measurable finger-strength gains in ~4 weeks in novices. Connective-tissue structural change (pulley, tendon, palmar-plate thickening) takes months-to-years.
- **[CONSENSUS] Structural finger adaptations documented cross-sectionally over years** (Schreiber et al. 2015, European J Sport Science). After ≥15 yr climbing: A2 pulleys 62–69% thicker, A4 69–76% thicker, DIP palmar plates 67–76% thicker vs non-climbers; flexor tendon differences smaller (1–21%). 10-yr follow-up: continued significant increase in pulley and flexor-tendon thickness; palmar-plate thickness plateaued. For tendon/bone, high load appears more effective than volume/frequency.
- **[CONTESTED] The "muscle outruns tendon" model is being questioned.** Smeets et al. (small, n=6, elderly total-knee-replacement patients) found ACL/patellar-tendon collagen protein-turnover rates similar to or greater than muscle — challenging the strict assumption that tendon adapts far slower than muscle. Contested and based on non-climbing, small samples.
- **[CONSENSUS] Collagen synthesis kinetics after loading** (Magnusson, Langberg & Kjaer): collagen formation peaks ~24 h post-exercise, remains elevated ~3 days; degradation also rises but peaks earlier, producing a **net-catabolic window in the first ~24–36 h** before net anabolism. Females show a blunted synthesis response (possibly estrogen-mediated).
- **Planning answer:** Finger-specific strength can outrun connective-tissue readiness over a window of **weeks to a few months** — strength/neural gains bank fast, structural pulley/tendon adaptation lags into months-to-years.
- **Open questions:** No in-vivo human pulley-adaptation timecourse; collagen-turnover debate unresolved for finger tissues specifically.

### A5. Hangboard and finger-strength protocols

- **[CONSENSUS] Structured hangboarding increases finger strength/endurance in trained climbers.** Best primary trial — López-Rivera & González-Badillo 2019, *J Hum Kinet* 66:183–195 (26 completers, mean 7c+/8a, 8 wk, 2×/wk): grip-endurance gains — **MaxHangs +34.1% (ES 0.6, p=0.010); intermittent/repeaters +45% (ES 1.0, p=0.002); combined Max_IntHangs only +7% (ES 0.1, ns)**. No significant between-group difference (small n). Authors' verbatim conclusion: "the intermittent dead-hangs training method seems to be more effective for grip endurance development after eight week application in advanced sport-climbers." No 95% CIs reported (only Hedges's g and p). The earlier 2012 study (n=9, mean 8a+/b) is paywalled and its strength-gain figure is inconsistent across secondary citations (1.34% author's blog; 9.6% Devise/Frontiers 2022; ~17% endurance per the 2019 paper) — **treat the 2012 strength effect as unverified**.
- **[CONSENSUS] Low-intensity ("Abrahangs") ≈ Max Hangs for strength, and effects are additive.** Gilmore et al. 2024, *Sports Med Open* 10:125 (retrospective Crimpd-app, n=526): Δ strength:weight — Climbing-only no change; **Abrahangs +2.5% (d=0.29, p=0.12 vs control); Max Hangs +3.2% (d=0.45, p=0.0005); Both +5.8% (d=0.79)**. No difference between Abrahangs-only and Max-Hangs-only; combined ≈ additive. Retrospective/non-randomized/self-reported — low internal validity; no 95% CIs.
- **[PRACTITIONER — convention ahead of evidence] Protocol specifics are convention, not validated.** The "7/3 repeaters × 6 = 1 set," "3–5 sets max hangs 7–10 s," 48–72 h spacing, 20 mm standard edge, and "~21.5% over 4 wk / 32% over 8 wk" (Anderson/Rock Prodigy, n=118 app users, self-reported) figures are practitioner conventions with weak evidential backing. **The hangboard literature is dominated by small (n<30) trials plus large uncontrolled app datasets; protocol prescriptions greatly exceed the evidence.**
- **[PRACTITIONER/CONTESTED] Appropriate training age for hangboarding.** Convention: build a base of ~6–12 months of climbing before structured hangboarding; avoid full-crimp and weighted hangs early; juniors <14 avoid max deadhangs; federations (British Mountaineering Council, UIAA MedCom) advise no campus/double-dyno before ~18 (skeletal immaturity — documented ~600% rise in youth epiphyseal fractures per Schöffl). The mechanistic "muscle outruns tendon" rationale for the beginner ban is contested; a better-supported explanation is that beginners add hangboard sessions ON TOP of climbing, spiking total finger-loading frequency (2–3 → 5–6 sessions/wk) — a volume-spike mechanism, not an intrinsic hangboard danger. One practitioner view holds hangboarding may be LOWER risk than hard bouldering (no dynamic shock-loading).
- **Open questions:** No RCT on hangboarding injury outcomes; no validated minimum training-age or base-period threshold; optimal frequency/intensity for connective-tissue safety untested.

### A6. Recovery and session frequency

- **[PRACTITIONER] Convention: 48–72 h between hard finger/bouldering sessions; 24–48 h after easy/technique sessions.** Widely repeated; the "48-h tendon recovery rule" is invoked but rests on general collagen-turnover data (A4), not climbing-specific recovery trials.
- **[CONSENSUS — mechanistic] The connective-tissue recovery window is distinct from and longer than muscular recovery.** Collagen synthesis is elevated ~72 h post-load with an early net-catabolic phase; this supports spacing hard finger loading ≥48 h, but the specific number is extrapolated.
- **[CONTESTED/PRACTITIONER] Optimal weekly structure unresolved.** Positions range from full supercompensation between sessions (traditional) to deliberate under-recovery for volume (high-volume block) with acknowledged higher injury risk. No controlled climbing study adjudicates.
- **Open questions:** No climbing RCT on session frequency vs injury; no validated finger connective-tissue recovery biomarker for athletes; deload/periodization for fingers untested.

## SECTION B — RESISTANCE TRAINING FOR INJURY PREVENTION IN THIS COMBINATION

### B1. Cycling-specific

- **[CONSENSUS] Anterior knee pain (PFPS) and patellar/quadriceps tendinopathy are the dominant cycling overuse complaints.** Per Dixit et al. / Clinics in Sports Medicine: "PFPS accounts for 25% to 40% of knee problems presenting to sports medicine clinics…and up to 25% of the knee problems in the general population." Anterior knee pain causes the greatest time-loss among cyclists.
- **[CONSENSUS — rehabilitation] For patellar tendinopathy, loading beats rest; isometric, heavy-slow-resistance (HSR), and eccentric all improve VISA-P.** Network meta-analysis (Li et al., Heliyon 2024, 7 studies, 1460 screened): eccentric-only ranked worst for VISA-P improvement; isometric best for short-term pain relief (analgesia persisting ~45 min); HSR and moderate slow resistance favored. **This is treatment evidence, not prevention evidence.**
- **[CONTESTED — prevention] Direct evidence that resistance training PREVENTS cycling knee overuse is essentially absent.** Prevention rationale is extrapolated from (a) general strength-training injury-prevention data (B4) and (b) tendinopathy rehab. **No cycling-specific RCT shows resistance training prevents incident anterior knee pain/patellar tendinopathy.** Bike-fit (saddle height/fore-aft, cleat, crank length) and cadence (>80–90 rpm, avoid big-gear grinding) have the strongest cycling-specific overuse-prevention support, and volume spikes (e.g., 100→150 km/wk) are a named trigger.
- **Transferable exercise selection/dosing (from tendinopathy loading literature):** HSR (~3–4 s concentric / 3–4 s eccentric, 6–15RM, 3×/wk) or isometric holds (~5×45 s at ~70% MVIC) for the quadriceps/patellar tendon; glute/hip strengthening to offload the patellofemoral joint.
- **Open questions:** No prevention RCT in cyclists; optimal prophylactic dose unknown; whether asymptomatic cyclists benefit from tendon loading untested.

### B2. Climbing-specific

- **[CONTESTED → toward CONSENSUS for shoulder/elbow] "Antagonist training" as injury prevention: real outcome evidence is limited to ONE cohort.** Two RCTs from a single cohort (Kozin/Kozina et al. 2021; 84 male students 18–19; intervention n=40 / control n=44; closed-kinematic-chain + eccentric + proprioceptive facilitation, 3–4×/wk for 1 yr) reduced shoulder and elbow injuries. Shoulder incidence-rate ratios: mild 0.861 (95% CI 0.737–1.007, ns), moderate 0.862 (0.751–0.990, p=0.039), severe 0.864 (0.768–0.971, p=0.017); all-shoulder rate control 3.182 vs intervention 0.5/1000 AEs (p<0.001). Elbow (companion RCT): odds mild 4.625× higher (CI 1.198–17.854) and moderate 5.588× higher (CI 1.143–27.324) in control. **Caveat: single young-male student cohort, moderate study quality, not generalizable to a mixed-age recreational combined athlete; the intervention was technique+eccentric+CKC, NOT isolated "push antagonist" work.**
- **[PRACTITIONER — convention ahead of evidence] The generic "train the push/antagonist muscles to prevent climbing injury" belief rests on mechanistic rationale + surrogate outcomes (posture, wrist-extensor strength ↔ grip), not injury-outcome data.** Practitioner sources themselves concede "the evidence for this claim in the literature is actually pretty sparse" and "antagonist strengthening alone won't prevent injury." **Widespread antagonist-training prescription is convention with thin injury-outcome backing; the only injury-outcome evidence is the single Kozin cohort, and it tested a broader CKC/eccentric program, not antagonist isolation.**
- **[CONSENSUS] Grip/finger strength deficit is itself a risk factor** (A3), so finger strengthening (not just antagonist) has a prevention rationale.
- **Open questions:** No RCT isolating antagonist/push training for climbing-injury outcomes; no dose-response; shoulder-girdle prevention program for adult recreational climbers untested.

### B3. Interference and interaction

- **[CONSENSUS] Concurrent strength+endurance interference is real but attenuated for cycling; cycling interferes with strength LESS than running.** Meta-analytic view: interference is body-part-specific (decrements in lower- not upper-body), and cycling-based endurance produces less hypertrophy/strength interference than running (running adds eccentric muscle damage). Schumann et al.: regardless of aerobic modality, frequency, age, or same-session vs different-day, concurrent training did NOT significantly impair hypertrophy or max strength — interference is smaller than once thought. Molecular window: after high-intensity endurance, AMPK returns to baseline in ~3 h; separating strength from prior endurance by ≥3 h reduces molecular interference; mTORC1 stays elevated ~18 h after strength.
- **[CONSENSUS — for this athlete] Cycling + lower-body strength can be SYNERGISTIC for cycling performance** (Aagaard et al.: concurrent added +8.4% mean power vs +3.7% endurance-only), reinforcing that the lower-body strength dose should be preserved.
- **[CONTESTED/NOVEL — the double-counting problem] Bouldering IS partly a maximal-strength stimulus for the upper limb and fingers.** Hard bouldering delivers near-maximal isometric finger and pulling loads — overlapping with what dedicated upper-body strength training provides. **Risk 1 (under-prescribing): assuming "climbing covers upper-body strength" can leave shoulder-girdle stabilizers, push antagonists, and posterior chain untrained** — precisely the tissues with prevention rationale (B2). **Risk 2 (double-counting / over-loading fingers): adding heavy hangboard/pulling strength ON TOP of hard bouldering stacks finger mechanical load** — the A3/A5 volume-spike mechanism. **Planning rule: count bouldering as upper-limb/finger maximal-strength load when deciding finger/pulling strength dose (avoid stacking), but do NOT count it toward lower-body, push/antagonist, or systemic-strength dose (which climbing does not supply).**
- **Open questions:** No study quantifies bouldering's contribution in strength-training "equivalents"; no validated method to net climbing load against prescribed finger-strength dose.

### B4. Longevity-relevant strength dosing

- **[CONSENSUS] Three doses diverge.**
  - **Mortality-benefit dose (lowest):** ~30–60 min/week of resistance training gives the maximum all-cause-mortality risk reduction. Momma et al. 2022 meta-analysis: any RT vs none — all-cause mortality RR 0.85 (15%↓), CVD 0.81 (19%↓), cancer 0.86 (14%↓); dose-response nonlinear, **max ~27% reduction at ~60 min/wk (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.64–0.86)**; benefit diminishes and may reverse above ~130–140 min/wk. Large 30-yr cohort (n=147,374): plateau around ≥120 min/wk; 90–119 min/wk all-cause mortality HR 0.87 (0.81–0.95); lowest mortality when combined with high aerobic activity (HR ~0.53–0.55).
  - **Injury-prevention dose (moderate-to-higher, dose-dependent, no known ceiling):** Lauersen et al. 2018 meta-analysis (BJSM, 6 RCTs, 7,738 athletes): strength training reduced sports injuries overall RR ≈ 0.34 (~66% reduction); overuse injuries roughly halved; **dose-dependent — higher volume/intensity → greater risk reduction, no observed harm ceiling**; no serious adverse events.
  - **Sport-performance dose (highest, sport-specific):** driven by concurrent-training gains and sport specificity; typically exceeds both above.
- **[CONSENSUS] The doses DIVERGE and one dose does NOT satisfy all three.** The mortality-optimal dose (~30–60 min/wk, ceiling ~120–140) is BELOW the injury-prevention optimum (dose-dependent) and below the performance optimum. **Because this athlete is longevity-led with injury-prevention as the operative constraint, the binding target is the injury-prevention dose, while noting mortality benefit is largely captured by the first ~60 min/wk.** There is no conflict up to ~120 min/wk (all three benefit); divergence appears above ~140 min/wk where mortality benefit flattens/reverses but injury-prevention and performance may continue.
- **Open questions:** Mortality dose-response rests on few studies (n=4 for the spline) and self-report; whether high-volume athletic strength training carries the same mortality flattening is untested.

### B5. Scheduling

- **[CONSENSUS] Intra-session sequence has only a minor effect on strength/hypertrophy** (meta-analyses: sequence not the leading driver of interference); ≥3 h separation between endurance and strength largely clears the molecular (AMPK) interference window.
- **[PRACTITIONER/CONSENSUS-mechanistic] Priority ordering for THIS athlete (finger-tissue-limited):**
  - Do NOT precede hard bouldering / max finger work with fatiguing pulling or hangboard work (compromises technique and stacks finger load → A3/A5 spike).
  - Separate hard finger sessions (bouldering + hangboard) by ≥48 h (A6 convention).
  - Heavy lower-body strength and hard cycling both load the knee/quads — avoid same-day stacking of high-knee-load work; if combined, cycling-then-strength or ≥3 h apart.
  - Low-intensity running (occasional) adds eccentric lower-limb load — keep away from heavy-leg-strength days and hard cycling days to limit cumulative knee/tendon load.
- **[CONTESTED — absence] No climbing-specific scheduling RCT exists** for ordering climbing vs strength vs cycling against injury outcomes. All ordering guidance here is mechanistic extrapolation.
- **Open questions:** No injury-outcome data on session ordering in multi-discipline athletes; minimum separation between hard bouldering and pulling-strength untested.

## Recommendations (Cross-Cutting) for the Planning System

*Each recommendation states the benchmark that would change it.*

1. **Track climbing load on a separate finger/upper-limb mechanical axis; do not sum into systemic endurance load.** Use sRPE × duration for whole-session internal load but flag it as under-capturing finger strain, especially for bouldering (validated r=0.40). *Changes if:* a validated finger-specific external-load metric or a validated climbing↔endurance conversion is published.
2. **Treat bouldering as a maximal upper-limb/finger strength stimulus in the accounting.** When prescribing finger/pulling strength, subtract bouldering load to avoid stacking; when prescribing lower-body, push/antagonist, and shoulder-girdle strength, assume climbing contributes ~nothing. *Changes if:* a study quantifies bouldering's strength-training equivalent for specific tissues.
3. **Space hard finger sessions (bouldering + hangboard) by ≥48 h; cap total finger-loading frequency.** Rationale: collagen synthesis elevated ~72 h with an early catabolic phase; no validated climbing recovery number exists. *Changes if:* a climbing recovery/frequency RCT or a finger connective-tissue recovery biomarker establishes a different window.
4. **Gate hangboarding on a climbing base (~6–12 months) and never ramp finger-load frequency abruptly; prefer adding hangboard by DISPLACING, not adding to, climbing finger volume.** The dominant injury mechanism is the total-finger-load spike, not the tool. *Changes if:* an RCT on hangboard injury outcomes or a validated training-age threshold appears.
5. **Do not rely on a specific climbing load-progression ramp threshold — none is validated.** Use conservative progression plus previous-injury and climbing-in-pain flags (strongest modifiable risk markers) instead. *Changes if:* a prospective climbing ACWR/ramp-rate study establishes a threshold.
6. **Set resistance-training dose to the injury-prevention target (dose-dependent, moderate-to-higher), not the mortality-minimum.** ~30–60 min/wk captures most mortality benefit; injury prevention (RR≈0.34, dose-dependent) and this athlete's longevity-led/injury-first objective justify more. Keep total structured strength ≤~120–140 min/wk to stay within the zone where mortality, injury, and performance benefits all align. *Changes if:* new mortality dose-response data extend or remove the ~120–140 min/wk flattening in athletes.
7. **Prioritize lower-body strength (knee/quad-tendon loading via HSR or isometrics) and shoulder-girdle/push strength — the tissues climbing does NOT train.** These carry the prevention rationale (cycling knee; climbing shoulder/elbow). *Changes if:* a cycling-specific prevention RCT or an adult recreational-climber shoulder-prevention RCT refines exercise selection.
8. **Exploit cycling×strength synergy; separate strength from prior hard endurance by ≥3 h; never precede hard bouldering with fatiguing pulling/hangboard.** *Changes if:* concurrent-training or climbing-scheduling injury-outcome data contradict the mechanistic ordering.
9. **Label antagonist/shoulder prevention work as rationale-based, not outcome-proven (single Kozin cohort).** Include it (low cost, mechanistic support) but do not over-weight it. *Changes if:* an independent RCT replicates injury-outcome benefit in adult recreational climbers.

## Caveats

- **Evidence-quality asymmetry.** The climbing literature is dominated by small cross-sectional/retrospective studies, self-reported/recall-based injury data, and a large practitioner-convention corpus (coaching blogs, training books) with thin evidential backing. Only two climbing injury-prevention RCTs exist (from one cohort), one validated internal-load study (n=15), and small (n<30) or uncontrolled finger-training trials. Resistance-training mortality/injury-prevention evidence is far stronger (meta-analyses of RCTs and large cohorts) but almost none is climbing- or cyclist-specific.
- **Extrapolated prevention claims.** Prevention of cycling knee overuse and climbing antagonist-training benefit are extrapolated from rehab/general-strength data, not directly demonstrated. Treat them as rationale-based.
- **Unverified/inconsistent figures.** The 2012 López-Rivera strength-gain percentage is inconsistent across secondary citations and was not verifiable from an open primary source. Finger-training trials report effect sizes and p-values, not 95% CIs; only the Kozin RCTs report CIs.
- **Population mismatch.** The Kozin injury-prevention RCT used 18–19-year-old male students; growth-plate data are pediatric. Generalization to a mixed-age recreational combined athlete is uncertain.
- **No speculative/future claims relied upon.** Where evidence is absent rather than negative (climbing ramp-rate thresholds, cycling prevention RCTs, hangboard injury-outcome RCTs, climbing scheduling RCTs), this is stated as "no evidence exists," not as a null finding.
