# Session-Level Exercise Prescription: Evidence Review (Review 3)

## TL;DR
- For **VO2max/peak-power gains in trained cyclists**, short on-off intervals (e.g., 3×13×30/15 s) outperform effort-matched long intervals (Rønnestad 2015: +8.7% vs +2.6% VO2max) **[CONTESTED]**; the "time near VO2max" convention is a useful organizing heuristic but only weakly predicts adaptation magnitude within-athlete **[CONTESTED]**. **Two quality sessions/week** is the practical default; a third yields no clear additional VO2max benefit in recreational athletes **[CONSENSUS]**.
- For **strength/tendon** in a longevity-led case: heavy load (>~80% 1RM) is required for maximal strength and for tendon stiffness (~90% MVC / 4.5–6.5% strain, 3-s holds, >12 wk); hypertrophy is load-flexible. Exercise **identity** matters less for whole-muscle growth than for region-specific architecture and for specific strength transfer **[CONSENSUS/CONTESTED]**.
- **Non-response to a given interval dose is largely abolished by increasing dose** (Montero & Lundby 2017): a flat review-period response should trigger a dose/density increase before a protocol switch **[CONSENSUS]**. Anchor intervals to **power (%FTP/CP) or RPE**, not HR, for efforts under ~2–3 min because of HR lag **[CONSENSUS]**.

## Section A — High-Intensity Interval Prescription (Cycling)

### A1. Protocol comparison
**[CONTESTED]** The best-known head-to-head is Rønnestad's short-interval (SI) vs effort-matched long-interval (LI) series. In well-trained cyclists (2015, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*), SI (3 series × 13 × 30 s work / 15 s recovery) improved VO2max +8.7% vs +2.6% for LI (4×5 min), with matched total effort. In elite cyclists (Rønnestad et al. 2020, same journal; 3-week, 9-session, VO2max 73 mL/kg/min), SI produced superior gains in peak power output and 20-min power, but **no significant between-group VO2max difference** — the advantage expressed as performance, not measured VO2max.

**[CONSENSUS]** At the modality level, meta-analyses find no clear winner between families in trained cyclists. The exploratory systematic review/meta-analysis of endurance-trained cyclists (IUSCA, 2023) found both HIIT and SIT effective, with effects vs control ranging from trivial (relative VO2max g=0.10, 95%CI −0.34–0.54) to small (absolute VO2max g=0.28, 0.15–0.40; MAP/PPO g=0.38, 0.15–0.61) to large but very imprecise (TT/TTE g=0.96, 95%CI −0.81–2.73), concluding interval prescription "cannot be solely explained by interval type or work-bout duration."

**[CONSENSUS]** SIT (30-s all-out type) in untrained/recreational adults: Sloth et al. (2013, meta-analysis, 13 effect sizes, 238 participants) reported weighted g=0.63 (95%CI 0.39–0.87), VO2max +4.2–13.4%. **Flag:** this rests heavily on short (2–8 week) studies in sedentary/recreationally active subjects — the known bias — and does not transfer cleanly to trained cyclists. A newer meta-regression (Hutchinson et al. 2026, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*, 93 trials/78 studies, n=1188) found the *number* of sprint repetitions per SIT session did **not** moderate VO2max change — arguing against "more sprints = more adaptation."

**Where head-to-heads are split/absent:** direct comparisons of sweet-spot vs threshold vs VO2max intervals on W' are largely absent; most W'-related work is descriptive (see A4/B). Threshold vs polarized comparisons exist (see B) but are distribution-level, not session-level.

### A2. The active-ingredient question
**[CONTESTED]** "Time ≥90% VO2max drives adaptation" is a convention traceable to Wenger & Bell (1986), Midgley et al. (2006) and Buchheit & Laursen (2013). The mechanistic logic (cardiac overload maximal near VO2max) is plausible, but the direct within-athlete test is weak. Odden et al. (2024, *Eur J Sport Sci*) measured oxygen uptake across 21 power-matched interval sessions over 9 weeks in well-trained cyclists and found that a higher *fraction* of VO2max during intervals correlated with performance gains; but earlier work (Rønnestad, Bjerkrheim et al. 2022) found time ≥90% VO2max did **not** correlate with improvements in maximal velocity or power at 4 mmol/L lactate. Reviews conclude the evidence is "ambiguous."

**[CONSENSUS on manipulations]** What increases time near VO2max: (a) shorter recoveries relative to work (30/15 accumulates more time ≥90% than 4×5 min); (b) "fast-start" pacing (Rønnestad cross-country skier studies: 90-s hard start then lower power raised peak VO2 and time >90% vs constant-pace); (c) adequate but incomplete recovery. **[CONTESTED]** Counter-evidence: intensified short 30-s intervals were *inferior* to traditional 3-min intervals for time >90% VO2max in runners (Thevenet-type protocols; 2019/2025 studies) because higher intensity reduced total repetitions (e.g., 11 vs 24 reps, ~65% less time >90% VO2max). So set structure interacts with intensity non-monotonically.

**Verdict:** treat time-near-VO2max as an *organizing convention* for designing sessions, not as a validated dose metric that predicts adaptation magnitude.

### A3. Dosing and frequency
**[CONSENSUS]** Two quality sessions/week is the practical default. Lenk, Matzka, Lauber, Kunz & Sperlich (2025, *Physiological Reports* 13:e70573; n=26 recreationally active, VO2max 51.3±7.1 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹, 6-wk once/twice/thrice-weekly design) concluded "2–3 weekly sessions of 4 × 4-min HIIT may be effective for improving VO2max and TTE...with no clear additional benefit from increasing the frequency to three sessions per week" (largest effects, d>0.5, in the two- and three-times groups). Rønnestad's cyclist series used 2–3 sessions/week.

**[CONSENSUS] Minimum effective dose:** Tjønna et al. (2013, *PLOS ONE* 8(5):e65382; 26 inactive overweight men, 10 wk) found a single 4-min bout at 90% HRmax 3×/week raised VO2max +10% (~5.0 mL·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹) vs +13% (~6.5) for 4×4-min (between-group p=0.08), concluding "a single bout of AIT performed three times per week may be a time-efficient strategy to improve VO2max." For *maintenance* vs *improvement*, the review-relevant finding is that increasing weekly dose progressively raises response rate (A5).

**[CONSENSUS] Interference with strength/climbing:** the interference effect is real but modest and highly conditional. Concurrent-training meta-analyses (Wilson et al. 2012; Schumann et al. 2021, n=43 studies) show strength/hypertrophy are largely preserved when endurance is ≤3×/week and sessions are separated; Wilson 2012 found interference with **running but not cycling** — directly relevant, since cycling is the target endurance modality. Placing strength before endurance within a session, or on separate days, minimizes attenuation; sequence is not the leading driver of interference over ≥8-week blocks.

### A4. Intensity anchoring
**[CONSENSUS]** HR is a lagging indicator (commonly cited as 30–60 s to stabilize), making it unsuitable for anchoring intervals shorter than ~2–3 min; power or RPE should steer short efforts. As one practitioner framing puts it, "if you're doing sprints that are less than 30 seconds, heart rate's pretty useless...the watts, if you've got a power meter...becomes the anchor." For steady threshold/sweet-spot efforts, HR is usable once stabilized.
- **%FTP/CP:** validated and practical for a power-meter athlete. VO2max intervals typically 106–120% FTP; sweet spot 84–94% FTP; threshold ~95–105% FTP. (Caveat: FTP is not a valid maximal-metabolic-steady-state marker — time-to-exhaustion at FTP averages ~33 min, not 60.)
- **%VO2max / %vVO2max:** used in lab-anchored protocols; requires testing.
- **RPE:** validated for effort-matching (Rønnestad's SI vs LI were RPE-matched); robust when HR/power are noisy (heat, fatigue, β-blockade — where HR falls but VO2 and work rate are preserved at a given RPE).
- **W'-balance-informed pacing:** validated for *predicting exhaustion* in intermittent efforts (Skiba 2012 model; Skiba et al. 2014 field validation in triathletes) and used to design sessions that fully deplete W' (SML-INT: +5% CP, +9%/4%/5% at 12/7/3-min power over 4 weeks). **Caveat:** Skiba & Clarke (2021, *IJSPP*) warn the model can predict recovery when none should occur outside tested conditions; reconstitution kinetics are curvilinear and highly individual (Bartram adjustment needed for elite cyclists).

**Practical default (power-meter athlete):** anchor VO2max/short intervals to power with RPE as live override; anchor threshold/sweet-spot to power confirmed by HR drift; use W'-balance only as a supplementary pacing check, not primary prescription. Head-to-head, HR- and power-anchored LT interval training produced statistically indistinguishable LT-power and 20-km TT gains in recreational cyclists.

### A5. Response heterogeneity
**[CONSENSUS]** HERITAGE Family Study (Bouchard et al. 1999, *J Appl Physiol*): 20 weeks standardized training in 481 sedentary adults, VO2max response ranged ~0 to >1.0 L/min (mean ~400 mL/min), with 2.5× more between-family than within-family variance; maximal heritability of the *response* ≈47%, maternal transmission ≈28%. Non-responders appeared at all ages, sexes and ethnicities.

**[CONSENSUS] Non-response is dose-dependent, not fixed.** Montero & Lundby (2017, *J Physiol* 595:3377–87): non-response prevalence fell from 69% → 40% → 29% → 0% → 0% at 60/120/180/240/300 min/week; adding 120 min/week for a further 6 weeks **abolished non-response universally**, driven largely by hemoglobin-mass gains (total Hb mass the strongest independent VO2max determinant, r_partial=0.49). Bonafiglia et al. (2016, *PLOS ONE*, crossover) showed individuals non-responsive to one modality (endurance vs SIT) often respond to the other — arguing protocol switching can rescue *some* non-response, but dose increase is the more robust lever.

**[PRACTITIONER→evidence-informed] Planning-system implication:** a flat VO2max/power response over a review period should first trigger a **dose/density increase** (add a session or volume) before concluding genuine non-response or switching protocol. Do not label an athlete a non-responder without having escalated dose.

**Open questions (A):**
- Does time ≥90% VO2max predict adaptation *within* trained cyclists, or only across heterogeneous groups? (unresolved)
- Do SI advantages hold beyond ~3–10 week blocks? (no long-duration trained-cyclist data — flag: short-study bias)
- Optimal frequency ceiling in masters athletes with concurrent strength/climbing (no direct evidence).

## Section B — Threshold and Subthreshold Work

### B1. Distinct stimulus vs "moderately hard filler"
**[CONTESTED]** Distribution-level trials favor polarized over threshold-heavy models in already-trained athletes: Neal et al. (2013, *J Appl Physiol*) ~8% peak-power gain (polarized) vs ~3% (threshold); Stöggl & Sperlich (2014, *Front Physiol*, 48 well-trained endurance athletes, 9 wk) found polarized improved VO2peak ~11.7% and outperformed threshold, HIIT and high-volume groups. This is the basis for "sweet spot is grey-zone" criticism. **However** these compared *distributions*, not isolated sessions, and comparison groups used threshold volumes no competent coach prescribes alone; the polarized advantage was real but contextual (already-trained subjects with substantial base volume).

**[CONSENSUS]** Threshold/sweet-spot work is efficient for time-crunched athletes: high stimulus per hour with lower fatigue cost than VO2max work, repeatable 2–3×/week. It drives power at fixed lactate concentrations and muscular endurance (pyramidal-distribution studies; Magalhães et al. 2024 PYR→POL sequence favored). What it does **not** clearly do that Z1 volume + HIIT does: there is no strong evidence sweet-spot produces a unique adaptation unobtainable from the combination of high easy volume plus true VO2max work — its value is time-efficiency, not a distinct physiological stimulus. **Flag:** "sweet spot" is heavily marketed; the coaching claim of a special adaptive window has outrun the controlled literature.

### B2. FTP/CP improvement specifically
**[CONSENSUS]** Multiple session types move sustainable power in trained cyclists:
- W'-depleting mixed intervals: +5% CP in 4 weeks (SML-INT), with +9%/+4%/+5% at 12/7/3-min mean maximal power.
- Threshold intervals (4×8 min type, 2×/week): improve power at OBLA (e.g., 222→239 W in one arm); TTE gains at 80% power@VO2max correlated with change in power at 4 mmol/L (r=0.66, p<0.001).
- HR- vs power-anchored LT intervals: both improved LT power +9.8% (~17 W) and 20-km TT time −7.8% over ~5 weeks in recreational cyclists (η²=0.63 for LT power; no anchor-method difference).
- **Heavy strength training** improves cycling performance via efficiency and anaerobic power (meta-analysis, PMID 40632222: efficiency ES=0.353, p=0.012; anaerobic power ES=0.560, p=0.024; performance ES=0.463, p=0.016) **without** changing VO2max, pVO2max or MLSS — relevant to the concurrent case (low certainty of evidence).

**Open questions (B):** No session-level RCT isolates sweet-spot vs threshold vs Z2-plus-HIIT for FTP/CP in trained cyclists with matched load; effect-size estimates for FTP are confounded by baseline training status and by FTP's questionable construct validity.

## Section C — Strength Session Content

### C1. Exercise selection
**[CONSENSUS] Load magnitude matters more than exercise identity for strength and tendon; whole-muscle hypertrophy is achievable by many exercises.** But:
- **Region/architecture specificity is real.** Zabaleta-Korta et al. (2021, *J Sports Sci* 39(20):2298–2304): leg extension grew rectus femoris (+8.8–19.7% across regions) where back squat did not (−2.2 to +3.0%), under matched volume/intensity (5-week — resistance-profile-confounded, authors' own caveat). Kubo et al. (2019, *Eur J Appl Physiol*): deep (0–140°) squats grew glute max (7% vs 2%) and adductors (6% vs 3%) more than half squats. Plotkin et al. (2023, *Front Physiol* 14:1279170): squat and hip thrust grew glutes similarly, but squat was superior for quads (Δ 3.6 cm², CI 0.7–6.4) and adductors (Δ 2.5, CI 1.2–3.9). Zabaleta-Korta et al. (2023, *J Hum Kinet*): only preacher curl (long muscle length) grew distal biceps significantly.
- **Strength transfer is highly exercise-specific** (Plotkin: squat→squat 3RM favored squat by 14±2 kg; hip thrust→hip thrust 3RM favored hip thrust by 26±5 kg). "Any progressive loading" does NOT suffice to maximize a *specific* strength outcome.
- **EMG activation does NOT predict hypertrophy** (Vigotsky et al. 2022, *Sports Med* 52(2):193–199: "acute...surface electromyograms [are not] a validated predictor of longitudinal hypertrophic adaptations"): do not select exercises on "activation" claims.
- **Exercise variation** should be systematic on anatomical/biomechanical grounds, not random (Kassiano et al. 2022, *JSCR*): redundant or excessive rotation adds no hypertrophy benefit.

**Tissue-specific evidence:**
- **Knee/quad tendon:** heavy slow resistance and high-strain loading are effective for healthy tendon (see C2). Isometrics: Rio et al. (2015/2017) evidence is for *pain relief in tendinopathy* (analgesia ~45 min post), not healthy-state stiffness — and it does not replicate at the Achilles (O'Neill et al. 2018) and failed to show analgesia in some patellar samples (Holden et al. 2019). For healthy-state stiffness, isometric vs dynamic does not matter (Bohm 2015); load magnitude does.
- **Posterior chain:** Nordic hamstring exercise lengthens biceps femoris long-head fascicles (distal, region-specific) and raises eccentric strength (+28%±20% high-volume, d=1.55; +34%±14% low-volume, d=2.09, over 6 wk; Ripley/Presland et al. 2018); even low volumes work (Cuthbert et al. 2020, *Sports Med*, meta-analysis). Architectural gains detrain within ~2 weeks; strength persists ≥4 weeks.
- **Hip/glute:** hip thrust and squat both grow glutes; choose by strength-transfer goal (Plotkin 2023).
- **Shoulder girdle / overhead press:** **no evidence exists** isolating exercise selection for healthy-adult strength/hypertrophy — any progressive overhead/push loading is defensible only by general principle.

### C2. Loading parameters (longevity-led)
**[CONSENSUS] Strength:** heavy load superior for maximal strength. Schoenfeld et al. (2017, *JSCR* 31(12):3508–3523, meta-analysis, 21 studies, all to failure): 1RM favored high load (>60% 1RM) ES=0.58 (95%CI 0.28–0.89, p=0.002); isometric strength no difference; **hypertrophy no difference** (ES diff 0.03, 95%CI −0.16–0.22). Heavy loads are required for the strength/retention goal; hypertrophy can be maintained across the loading spectrum provided sets approach failure.

**[CONSENSUS] Tendon stiffness/CSA (healthy):** Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis (2015, *Sports Med – Open* 1:7, meta-analysis, 27 studies/37 interventions/264 participants): stiffness SMD=0.70 (95%CI 0.51–0.88), Young's modulus 0.69 (0.36–1.03), CSA 0.24 (0.07–0.42). Adaptation depends on **loading intensity, not contraction type** ("loading magnitude in particular plays a key role...in contrast to muscle contraction type"). Optimal stimulus (Arampatzis/Bohm intervention series; e.g., *J Exp Biol* 2007, 2014): ~90% MVC / **4.5–6.5% tendon strain, 3-s contractions**, ~5×4 reps, >12 weeks; low strain (~3%) does not adapt tendon. Major stiffness change occurs within the first ~8 weeks.

**[CONSENSUS] Aging tendon/strength:** heavy RT increased patellar tendon stiffness and Young's modulus in older adults (HRT +21% isometric MVC) where moderate load (MRT +8%) and controls did not; CSA increased equally with moderate and heavy load (Beyer/Eriksen older-adult trial). Létocart et al. (2024, *Scand J Med Sci Sports*, n=27 elderly men): both moderate (55% 1RM) and heavy-progressive (to 80% 1RM) increased patellar/Achilles tendon CSA (+1.7–2.8%), but stiffness/Young's modulus increases were site-dependent. **Load magnitude is the key driver at all ages.**

**[CONSENSUS] Bone:** resistance training improves BMD in older adults (multiple meta-analyses, PROSPERO-registered); ≥2 sessions/week of higher-load or high-velocity RT positively affects lumbar spine, femoral neck and total hip BMD; gains reverse without continued loading.

### C3. Minimum effective strength dose
**[CONSENSUS] Strength maintenance/building:** low volume preserves and can still build strength. Androulakis-Korakakis et al. (2021, *Front Sports Act Living*): METD for 1RM in trained powerlifters ≈ **3–6 working sets/week/lift, 1–5 reps, >80% 1RM, RPE 7.5–9.5, 6–12 weeks**; the earlier systematic review showed a single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week produced sub-optimal but statistically significant 1RM gains. Twice-weekly frequency beats once-weekly for strength/hypertrophy.
- **Hypertrophy maintenance:** ~6–10 sets/muscle/week maintains; possibly less during short cuts.
- **Tendon:** requires the high-strain stimulus to be preserved — low-load high-rep work maintains muscle but is insufficient for tendon; the high-strain exposure is what must survive cutting last.

**What survives cutting a session short (evidence-informed):** keep (1) the heavy compound at target load — strength and tendon depend on load magnitude, few sets suffice; drop (2) accessory/isolation volume first — hypertrophy tolerates volume reduction; (3) preserve at least one high-strain exposure per target tendon.

**Open questions (C):** METD studies are dominated by young/male powerlifters; women and masters athletes are under-studied. No healthy-state exercise-selection RCTs exist for shoulder/overhead. Tendon-loading trials rarely exceed 14 weeks.

## Section D — Progression Logic

### D1. Within-block progression
**[CONTESTED/absent]** There is essentially **no session-level ramp-rate evidence for interval work** analogous to endurance load ramp-rate. Practitioner conventions (e.g., 4×6→4×8→24 min at equal power, or repeating a session and raising average power) are folklore without controlled support. The closest evidence: McNicol et al. (progressive vs constant run intensity, 6 weeks, previously sedentary) found progressively increasing intensity produced greater VO2peak and lactate-inflection gains than holding intensity constant — supporting *some* progression, but not a specific rate. ACSM progression guidelines are explicitly acknowledged in the literature as "vague and not supported by strong evidence."

**[CONSENSUS] Strength progression:** load and repetition progression produce equivalent hypertrophy when effort/volume are matched (Plotkin et al. 2022) — what should progress is effort/proximity to failure and total volume, not necessarily absolute load. The "10%/week" rule is practitioner convention, not derived from RCT.

### D2. Plateau handling
**[CONSENSUS, best-supported]:** **increase dose/density** — Montero & Lundby (2017) shows added weekly volume abolishes non-response; block-periodization density (Rønnestad et al. 2014) raised VO2max where matched even-spaced sessions did not, despite identical total HIIT sessions.
**[CONTESTED]:** **protocol switch** — Bonafiglia (2016) supports modality switching rescuing some individuals, but it is a weaker lever than dose.
**[PRACTITIONER]:** **deload** — widely used, physiologically reasonable (fatigue masking fitness), but lacks direct RCT support at the session level for breaking VO2max plateaus.
**[PRACTITIONER/limited evidence]:** **intensity increase at constant volume** — some support (McNicol; higher-intensity raised responder rate at matched energy expenditure, Sports Med Open 2023) but confounded with dose.

**Order of operations for a flat response:** (1) verify easy days are truly easy and sessions are being completed at target; (2) increase density/volume; (3) if still flat over a further block, switch protocol family; (4) deload only if fatigue markers indicate masking.

**Open questions (D):** No session-level ramp-rate RCTs for intervals; deload efficacy for plateau-breaking is untested; the optimal block length for VO2max concentration (commonly cited 4–8 weeks) is practitioner convention.

## Recommendations for the Planning System
1. **Default cycling VO2max block:** two quality sessions/week, short intervals (30/15 or 40/20) anchored to power (~106–120% FTP) with RPE override, in 4–6 week concentrated blocks. *Benchmark that would change it:* a within-athlete A/B showing long intervals produce ≥ equal VO2max/PPO gain at lower session RPE.
2. **Anchor short intervals to power/RPE, never HR** (efforts <2–3 min). *Change if:* athlete lacks a power meter — then RPE + terrain, not HR.
3. **Treat a flat review-period response as under-dosing first.** Escalate volume/density before switching protocol or labeling non-response. *Change threshold:* if two successive dose escalations (≈+120 min/week equivalent, per Montero & Lundby) fail to move VO2max/CP beyond typical measurement error, then switch protocol family.
4. **Strength: prescribe heavy compound loading (>80% 1RM) for the key lifts** to serve strength retention and tendon; treat hypertrophy volume as the cuttable buffer. *Change if:* joint tolerance limits heavy loading — then moderate load to failure preserves hypertrophy, but flag that the tendon/max-strength stimulus is reduced.
5. **Preserve one high-strain tendon exposure per target tendon** (~90% MVC / 4.5–6.5% strain, 3-s holds) when time-constrained. *Change if:* symptomatic — but that is rehab, out of this review's scope.
6. **Protect strength from interference** by scheduling strength before cycling HIIT or on separate days, keeping cycling HIIT ≤3×/week. *Change if:* strength stalls despite this — reduce concurrent endurance frequency (interference from cycling is smaller than from running, so this athlete is comparatively protected).
7. **Do not prescribe sweet-spot as a distinct adaptive stimulus** — use it only as a time-efficiency tool in constrained weeks. *Change if:* a matched-load session-level trial demonstrates a unique sweet-spot adaptation.
8. **Progress effort/volume, not a fixed weekly load percentage** — there is no validated session-level ramp rate. *Change if:* controlled interval ramp-rate evidence emerges.

## Caveats
- **Short-study / untrained-subject bias:** much SIT and HIIT effect-size data (Sloth 2013; Tjønna 2013; Lenk 2025; many meta-analyses) rests on 2–10 week studies in sedentary/recreational subjects; transfer to trained masters cyclists is assumed, not demonstrated.
- **Rønnestad SI>LI findings** come from short (3–10 week) blocks; durability and generalization beyond his group are unconfirmed, and the elite result showed no VO2max difference.
- **Tendon and strength meta-analyses** carry low-to-moderate certainty (Schoenfeld 2017 heterogeneity I²≈80% for strength; heavy-strength-for-cycling explicitly "low certainty"). Healthy-tendon parameters derive largely from Arampatzis/Bohm's own intervention series.
- **Several tendon-loading protocols cited in secondary sources are tendinopathy (rehab) studies** (Kongsgaard 2009, Beyer 2015, Rio 2015/2017) — excluded from healthy-state conclusions except where healthy-tendon data (Bohm 2015; Kongsgaard 2007) exist.
- **W'-balance models** are validated for exhaustion prediction under tested conditions only; extrapolation to novel work:recovery structures is cautioned by the model's own authors (Skiba & Clarke 2021).
- **Coaching folklore flagged as outrunning evidence:** interval-pyramid progression rules, "time at VO2max" as a validated dose metric, sweet-spot as a unique stimulus, %1RM orthodoxy for hypertrophy, and the 10%/week progression rule. Where the literature is silent rather than negative — session-level interval ramp rate, shoulder/overhead exercise selection, deload for plateau-breaking — "no evidence exists" is stated explicitly.
